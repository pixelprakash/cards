Design as inquiry

Futures require
what does not
yet exist.

In the context of accelerating global challenges, higher education needs frameworks that move beyond incremental reform. Pedagogy Playcards is an ongoing, card-based speculative tool for generating those futures — because a future, by definition, is something that does not exist yet.

By drafting combinations of places, values, actions, and modes, participants construct possibility spaces where the status quo of learning can be questioned and reconfigured. The scenarios that surface aren't predictions; they're provocations — surfacing the tensions, aspirations, and uncertainties inside multiple pedagogical futures at once.

Games function as social systems: they represent reality while simultaneously constructing new social structures. It's a premise drawn from Johan Huizinga's Homo Ludens (1938), which frames play as something fundamental to being human — not a break from serious thought, but a method for it.

"The future exists first in imagination, then in will, then in reality." — Barbara Marx Hubbard, Futurist & author
The deck

Five elements, one drafting table.

Each deck holds a different kind of building block. Combined on a drafting template, they generate draft narratives that explore alternative educational futures — and every deck is open enough to extend with your own cards.

Worked example
Apply + Lifelong Learning + Flipped Classroom + Artisanal Marketplace

"Picture a night market where retirees teach teenagers to bargain in five languages — and the going rate for a lesson is a home-cooked meal."

A drafted prompt: one possible reading of this combination, written by a group of participants at the table. No two drafts read the same way twice.

1st
Playtest, Delhi

Nineteen students and three teachers played the first rough draft of the game in Delhi. Since then it has travelled through multiple situations, contexts, and age groups — the card counts have changed several times along the way.

Initial drafts were often humorous. Repeated iterations revealed increasingly imaginative and meaningful outcomes, giving rise to design directions that stay inaccessible when educational research remains rooted only in the present:

Anti-disciplinary knowledge clusters Quantum pedagogy Failure-Based Learning Models

A suspension of disbelief, it turns out, is essential — for participants to engage fully with speculative combinations, and to keep pushing past what learning is currently assumed to be.

From the research

If a thesis can be a possibility space, so can an education.

The same inquiry that shapes this deck extends beyond the drafting table — into a companion piece on rethinking research assessment itself, Temporal, Multimodal, and Open: Research Assessment in the Age of AI, from the same lab.

01

Wiki-theses — public platforms where students build, revise, and present research collectively, as evolving portfolios rather than single final documents.

02

Failure-first models — documenting dead-ends and unviable hypotheses, valuing resilience over optimised, AI-polished outcomes.

03

Multimodal & longitudinal theses — video, simulation, and performance standing alongside text, tracking the research journey rather than only its final form.

04

Algorithmically co-authored work — formalising AI's role in research. Wicked Problems: What Can We Do in This Time of Collapse? (2025) credits ClimateGPT as co-author on its cover, chapter by chapter.
